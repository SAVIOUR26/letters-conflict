## Chapter 4 · Stone, Sea, and Bronze  (Part I · notes start at 57)

**Job:** widen “civilisation” to southern Africa, the Swahili coast and Ife/Benin — builders, merchants, artisans — and show different forms of collective meaning.

- **Great Zimbabwe’s economy and society** (not its authorship — Ch 1 owns that): cattle, gold, trade towards the coast (Sofala), households and local production. Source already cited: Chirikure 2020 [4]; UNESCO [2]. Look also at Innocent Pikirayi’s work.
- **Mapungubwe** (South Africa, UNESCO site): the golden rhinoceros; dating c. 1220–1290; class division on the hill; precursor to Great Zimbabwe.
- **Kilwa and the Swahili coast:** Ibn Battuta’s 1331 visit and praise of Kilwa; Husuni Kubwa palace; Kilwa coinage; the Kilwa Chronicle; Swahili as an African language with Arabic loanwords (Bantu grammar). UNESCO Kilwa [21] already cited.
- **Ife:** the naturalistic copper-alloy and terracotta heads (Wunmonije compound finds, 1938). **[Correction]** Leo Frobenius (1910) claimed Ife art proved a lost Greek/Atlantis colony — the Great Zimbabwe pattern again, but in bronze.
- **Benin:** the Oba, the guild of bronze-casters (Igun Eronmwon), the city walls and moats. **[Correction]** the popular “Benin walls were four times longer than the Great Wall of China” claim (often traced to a 1999 *New Scientist* piece / Patrick Darling) — check what the survey actually measured.
- The 1897 removal of the Benin Bronzes belongs to Ch 10 — only a forward reference here.
- **Who Carried the Weight:** labour and hierarchy at Mapungubwe/Great Zimbabwe; the slave trade on the Swahili coast; Benin’s court economy and captives.
- **Letter Principle idea:** different arrangements (stone towns, coastal merchant cities, artisan guilds) produced different kinds of meaning; no single ladder of civilisation.

## Chapter 5 · The Grammar of African Power  (Part I)

**Job:** compare centralised and decentralised societies — how they distributed authority, land and accountability. Begin the Great Lakes thread.

- **Buganda:** Kabaka, Katikkiro, Lukiiko, clans (bataka) and their totems, saza chiefs; Kabaka Mutesa I’s court (Stanley’s 1875 visit, already cited [13]); Apolo Kagwa’s *Basekabaka be Buganda* (1901) — Ganda history written by a Muganda.
- **Bunyoro-Kitara:** older kingdom; Omukama; Kabalega and resistance (resistance itself belongs to Ch 7).
- **Kooki:** `[AUTHOR: history of Kooki, its relationship with Buganda (reported 1896 agreement — verify), its institutions, what elders teach]`. Do not guess; leave placeholders.
- **Decentralised societies:** the Igbo saying “Igbo enwe eze” (the Igbo have no king), age grades and village assemblies; E. E. Evans-Pritchard’s “ordered anarchy” among the Nuer (1940) — use carefully.
- **Asante:** the Golden Stool as a symbol of the nation above any individual king (useful for the trilogy’s “office larger than the officer” theme).
- **Ecology and density:** Jeffrey Herbst, *States and Power in Africa* (2000) — control of people rather than land; Marcella Alsan, “The Effect of the TseTse Fly on African Development,” *AER* 2015.
- **Michalopoulos & Papaioannou**, “Pre-colonial Ethnic Institutions and Contemporary African Development,” *Econometrica* 2013 — present as evidence, not as a moral ranking.
- **Who Carried the Weight:** slavery and clientship inside kingdoms; women’s roles and limits; the 1886 killings of Christian converts at Mutesa’s successor Mwanga’s court (handle with care; verify).
- **[Correction]** “Africans were tribes, not states” — or its reverse, “every African society was a kingdom.”

## Chapter 6 · The Markets of Captivity  (Part II · What Was Broken)

**Job:** the several slave trades, African and external actors, resistance, and the long-run damage to trust.

- **Signature scene:** King Afonso I (Nzinga Mbemba) of Kongo’s letters to João III of Portugal, 1526, protesting the trade — literally letters in conflict. Find a published translation.
- **Scale:** SlaveVoyages database (≈12.5 million embarked across the Atlantic, 1501–1866 — confirm current estimate); trans-Saharan and Indian Ocean trades.
- **Nathan Nunn**, “The Long-Term Effects of Africa’s Slave Trades,” *QJE* 2008; **Nunn & Wantchekon**, “The Slave Trade and the Origins of Mistrust in Africa,” *AER* 2011 — the mistrust mechanism: “before a letter could fear the stranger, it learned to fear the neighbour.”
- Paul Lovejoy, *Transformations in Slavery*.
- **Resistance:** Queen Nzinga of Ndongo and Matamba; shipboard revolts; communities that moved to defensible terrain.
- **East Africa:** Zanzibar, Tippu Tip, the nineteenth-century ivory and slave caravans into the Great Lakes region.
- **[Correction]** Olaudah Equiano’s African birth (questioned by Vincent Carretta) — or the slogan “Africans sold Africans” vs “Europeans did everything”: show the system of incentives and the many different actors.
- **Letter Principle idea:** when a system rewards betrayal more than cooperation, moral speeches alone cannot make the letters cooperate.

## Chapter 7 · Lines Drawn Across Living Worlds  (Part II)

**Job:** move beyond the map — how conquest changed authority, labour, tax and land in particular places.

- **[Correction]** The Berlin Conference (1884–85) did not sit down and draw Africa’s borders on a map; it set rules (e.g. “effective occupation”). Most borders were drawn later by bilateral treaties. Verify.
- Michalopoulos & Papaioannou, “The Long-Run Effects of the Scramble for Africa,” *AER* 2016 — partitioned homelands; ~28% figure must be checked.
- **Uganda thread:** the 1900 Buganda Agreement (Sir Harry Johnston; regents including Apolo Kagwa); mailo land; Buganda agents such as Semei Kakungulu used to administer other regions; hut and poll taxes.
- **Resistance:** Yaa Asantewaa’s war (1900); Maji Maji (1905–07); Kabalega of Bunyoro.
- **Indirect rule:** Lugard, *The Dual Mandate* (1922); research showing Africans and colonial officials jointly reshaped “Native Authorities” (Cambridge JEH paper on local taxation, cited in the research foundation PDF).
- Development and extraction together: railways (Uganda Railway), forced labour, cash crops.

## Chapter 8 · Freedom with Inherited Machinery  (Part III · What Was Inherited)

**Job:** separate inherited constraints from African decisions at independence.

- Ghana 1957 (Nkrumah) as comparison.
- **Uganda:** independence 9 October 1962; the 1966 crisis (Obote, the attack on the Lubiri, Kabaka Mutesa II’s exile); the 1967 constitution abolishing the kingdoms; their restoration in 1993.
- OAU 1964 Cairo resolution accepting colonial borders — Africans choosing to keep the inherited map.
- Inherited civil services, colonial-era laws, economic geography (export crops, railway lines to ports).
- NEPAD’s 2001 diagnosis (colonial legacy + Cold War + international system + post-independence policy failures) — useful because it refuses both total external and total internal blame.

## Chapter 9 · When Power Became Greater Than Purpose  (Part III)

**Job:** one-party states, coups, Cold War patrons, structural adjustment — and the counter-example of continuity.

- Coups from the 1960s (Togo 1963, Ghana 1966, Uganda 1971) — verify dates and characterise carefully.
- Cold War patronage (e.g. Mobutu); APSR paper “Elite Change without Regime Change” (cited in research foundation).
- Structural adjustment in the 1980s.
- **Botswana** as a complication (Acemoglu, Johnson & Robinson, “An African Success Story,” 2003).
- The 1990s wave of multiparty reforms.
- **[Correction]** “neopatrimonialism explains all African politics” — show the scholarly critique of over-broad use.
- Keep Book Two’s territory clear: this chapter explains where personalised power came from historically; Book Two dramatises how it feels today.
